LEAPS calls, in plain English

A ten-minute guide to the strategy the checker scores: what a LEAPS call is, how to pick one, how to manage it, and what can go wrong. Videos and reference links are at the end.

What is a LEAPS call?

LEAPS stands for Long-term Equity AnticiPation Securities. They are ordinary listed options with a long life: more than a year, and up to about three years, to expiry.

A call gives you the right, but not the duty, to buy 100 shares at a fixed price (the strike) until the expiry date. One contract covers 100 shares, so a call quoted at $28.00 costs $2,800.

The LEAPS call strategy uses one as a cheaper stand-in for owning the shares. You get most of the stock's moves for a fraction of the money, and the most you can lose is what you paid.

Why deep in the money?

The checker looks for calls with a strike well below today's price ("in the money"), with a delta of about 0.75 to 0.85. Delta is roughly how much the option moves when the stock moves $1. At 0.80, a $1 rise in the stock adds about $0.80 to the call.

  • It behaves more like the stock. A high-delta call follows the shares closely. A cheap, far out-of-the-money call needs a big move just to break even.
  • Less paid for time. Most of a deep in-the-money call's price is real value (the stock price minus the strike). The rest, the time value, shrinks to zero by expiry. A smaller share of time value means less to lose to decay.
  • Long expiry, slow decay. Time value melts slowly at first and fast in the last months. Buying 12 to 24 months out, and selling or rolling about 9 months before expiry, keeps you out of the fast part.

A worked example

Say a stock trades at $100. You could buy 100 shares for $10,000, or buy one call with a $75 strike, 18 months out, for $28 a share ($2,800).

The call ties up $2,800 instead of $10,000 and earns a bigger percentage when the stock rises. It also loses a bigger percentage when the stock falls, and the whole $2,800 can be lost. The break-even at expiry is the strike plus what you paid: $75 + $28 = $103. (Figures are rounded and assume nothing else changes, such as volatility.)

A common rule: keep any one LEAPS position to 2 to 5% of your portfolio, so a total loss is a setback, not a disaster.

How to pick one: the eight checks

These are the rules the checker scores on every analysis.

  1. Time: 12 to 24+ months to expiry, so you have time to be right and decay stays slow.
  2. Delta: about 0.75 to 0.85: deep enough to act like the stock, without paying for strikes far below the price.
  3. Break-even: close to today's price, so the stock doesn't need a big move just to get your money back.
  4. Time value and volatility: pay little for time, and avoid buying when implied volatility is unusually high, because options are expensive then.
  5. A tight market: a small gap between bid and ask, and plenty of open contracts, so you can get in and out at a fair price.
  6. The stock itself: a business you'd be happy to own, in an uptrend or with a clear bottom.
  7. Position size: 2 to 5% of your portfolio.
  8. An exit plan, set before you buy: see the next section.

Hold, sell or roll

The checker's exit plan, which the "Check a call you own" box also uses:

  • Take profit at +50% (part of the position) to +100% (the whole thing).
  • Cut the loss at about −40%, especially if the reason you bought (often the stock's uptrend) has broken.
  • Sell or roll about 9 months before expiry, before time decay speeds up.
  • Earnings and big news can move a stock sharply overnight. Know the dates before you buy.

What "rolling" means

Rolling is two trades: sell the call you own and buy a later one, often at a higher strike. You keep your stake in the stock without riding an old call into its fast-decay months. Rolling up to a higher strike after a big gain also takes money off the table.

The risks

  • You can lose everything you paid. If the stock is below the strike at expiry, the call expires worthless.
  • Leverage cuts both ways. A 20% drop in the stock can mean a 50% or larger drop in the call.
  • Time works against you. Even a flat stock costs you the time value.
  • Volatility matters. If implied volatility falls after you buy, the call can lose value even if the stock doesn't move.
  • No dividends. Call holders don't receive the stock's dividends, and the share price usually drops by the dividend on the ex-dividend date.
  • Wide spreads cost money. Use limit orders near the middle of the bid and ask, never market orders.

Before trading options in the US, your broker must give you the official risk booklet, Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options (linked below). It's worth reading.

Words to know

Strike
The price at which the call lets you buy the shares.
Expiry
The last day the option exists.
Premium
The price of the option, quoted per share (× 100 per contract).
In the money
For a call, a strike below the stock price.
Intrinsic value
Stock price minus strike: what the call would be worth if it expired today.
Time value
The rest of the premium. It shrinks to zero by expiry.
Delta
About how much the option moves for a $1 move in the stock.
Theta
About how much value the option loses each day from time decay.
Implied volatility (IV)
How much movement the option price assumes. High IV means expensive options.
Open interest
How many contracts are open. More means an easier market to trade.
Roll
Sell your option and buy a later one in its place.
